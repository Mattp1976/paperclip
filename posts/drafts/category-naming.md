# Headcount

*Draft. Not published. Sitting here so the vocabulary is mine the moment I need it.*

---

The first time I tried to describe what I was building, I reached for "AI workflow tool" and instantly knew it was wrong. Workflow is what you call the boxes-and-arrows software that automates a known process. What I was actually building was a place where a person could hire a CEO, give her a task, and watch her get it done — sometimes by doing it herself, sometimes by telling someone else on the team to do it. That's not a workflow. That's a company.

The category we're inventing — and most of us are inventing it without admitting it — is the operating system for an AI organisation. And the reason it's hard to talk about is that we keep borrowing words from the wrong places. We say "agent" and mean a script. We say "team" and mean a chat thread. We say "automation" and mean a person we just chose not to hire. The vocabulary is dragging the thinking backward.

Here are the four words I think are doing real work, and why.

## Fleet

The collective noun for the agents you've stood up. Not "your agents" (sounds like assets), not "your team" (sounds like humans), not "your automations" (sounds like cron). A fleet is a set of capable units operating under a single command. You can grow it, prune it, retire ships, recommission others. You can have one fleet or several. You can install a fleet from a template — five content people in one click — and then customise it.

The reason this word matters: it forces you to think in plural. The unit of analysis isn't the agent, it's the fleet. You don't ask "what is this agent doing?" — you ask "what is my fleet shipping this week?" The single-agent framing makes you build assistants. The fleet framing makes you build a company.

## Standup

The daily roll-up of what your fleet did, is doing, and is blocked on. Three buckets per agent: closed, in flight, and in the way. The same three columns a human PM has been running standups with for thirty years. We didn't need a new word — we needed to admit the old one applies.

This matters because the moment you call it a standup, you stop building dashboards. Dashboards are passive. A standup has an audience and a rhythm. It runs at 08:00 whether you read it or not. It tells you who's stuck. The work fits into your morning the way a standup with humans would — except the agents have already worked through the night, and you're catching up to them rather than the other way round.

## Delegation graph

The structure of who reports to whom and who hands work to whom. Not the org chart (org charts are static; delegation graphs are alive), not the workflow diagram (workflows describe steps, graphs describe relationships), not the call graph (call graphs are about code).

Every interesting agent system is a delegation graph in disguise. The CEO routes to the Research Director. The Research Director hands a brief to the Researcher. The Researcher comes back with sources. The CEO summarises for the human. That's a graph with edges that mean *I gave this to you and I'm waiting on you.* When you can see the graph — actually see it, drawn — you can find the bottleneck. That bottleneck is almost always an agent doing too much, or a human still being routed work that an agent should have absorbed.

If you can't draw your delegation graph, you don't have an organisation; you have a chat history.

## Headcount

Here's the one I expect arguments about. Headcount is the count of agents in your fleet — and yes, I'm using a word that we've used for humans for a hundred years to describe software. That's the point.

You measure a company by how many people work there. The number is a proxy for capacity, for cost, for management overhead, for ambition. As soon as agents have names and titles and report to one another and rack up costs and produce shipped work, the same proxy applies. The CFO of an agent-driven company is going to ask "what's our headcount?" and mean it literally.

The pushback will be: but they're not people. Correct. They're not. They also have a name, a title, a manager, a budget, an inbox, and a record of what they've shipped. If it walks like a company, you may as well count it like one. The alternative is "active agent instances," which nobody is ever going to say out loud.

## Why I'm writing this down

Words shape what you can build. We spent a decade calling agents "bots" and got chat experiences. We spent five years calling them "copilots" and got productivity assistants. If we keep calling fleets "automations," we'll get cron with a chat overlay.

If we call them companies, we'll build companies.

The four words above — *fleet*, *standup*, *delegation graph*, *headcount* — already sound right when you say them out loud about agent systems. They sound less right when you say them about workflows or automations or copilots, which tells you exactly which category they actually belong to. They are the vocabulary of organisations.

So when the moment comes — and it's coming — to name what we're all building, those are the words I'll use. I'm writing them down now so I don't reach for the wrong ones later.
